The short answer

Yes, the UI side of the palette has too many colours, but not because the game has too many meanings. The excess is near-duplicate greys and accent shades that repeat each other. Players read the whole board as roughly eight UI roles (panel greys, cursor, positive, negative, caution, EXP, bonus, inactive) plus two labelled reference keys: the Pokemon types and the status chips.

The research points to three numbers. Keep the UI chrome to about 30-35 colours, which matches commercial pixel HUD kits that lock the whole UI to one 32-colour palette [1]. Use 7-8 greys, one per job. And keep the colours a player must read at a glance to about six hues, because colour alone tops out near seven quickly-distinguished categories [2].

One caveat runs through all of this. No source publishes a colour count for a survivor-like HUD or for the Black 2 / White 2 battle screen, so the 30-35 target is an inference from design systems, pixel UI kits and perception research, not a measured norm for this genre.

Count roles, not tokens

The most useful idea in the research is that a palette has two sizes. The token list is what the code maintains. The role count is what the player perceives. Material Design 3 builds this split into its model: a few key colours expand into tonal palettes, and tones are then assigned to roles that the UI actually uses [3]. Radix does the same within a single hue: its 12 steps each have one job, so a user sees one hue doing several jobs rather than twelve colours [4].

The big libraries look enormous because they are menus, not palettes. Tailwind ships 11 steps for every colour [5], and Radix has about 372 styles per mode across its scales [6]. A real product picks one neutral, one to three accent hues, and about four semantic hues (success, warning, error, info) from them.

The one published before-and-after audit is the closest match to this board. UXPin found 116 colour variables in its product UI [8], including 62 shades of grey [9]. Their warning sign was the same one the All Colours tab now shows: colours used only once that sit right next to an existing grey [8].

The familiar 60-30-10 rule (60% dominant, 30% secondary, 10% accent) also appears in indie game advice [10], but it describes how much screen area each colour covers, not how many tokens to keep. For a dark HUD it reads naturally as: dark panels dominate, rims and text are secondary, and saturated colours are the small accent.

Pixel art and the DS look

The HUD copies Black 2 / White 2, so the DS hardware is the natural reference. The Pokemon Platinum decompilation documents the DS 2D engine as supporting 16 palettes of 16 colours each, with 256 colours on screen [11]. With 16-colour tiles, each tile draws from one palette [12]. So a single BW2 panel element was very likely built from 15 colours or fewer plus transparency. That is an inference from the hardware; no source counts the colours in the BW2 battle HUD itself.

Pixel-art practice agrees on small ramps. A ramp is typically three to five hue-shifted shades from dark to light [13], and small canvases need fewer, with shared colours keeping the total down [14]. One artist's guide puts it bluntly [15]. The light / base / dark rims already follow this. Whole scenes usually sit at 8 to 32 colours [16], and well-known palettes come in sizes of 4, 16 and 32 [17].

Shipped games treat limits as a guide, not a cage. Shovel Knight kept to the NES's 54 colours and added four more for detail and darker skin tones [18], aiming for a "rose-tinted" NES rather than an exact copy [19]. The lesson for this board is that a few deliberate extra colours are fine; many accidental ones are not.

Celeste's artist describes "full consistency" as doing everything at one resolution [20]. Applied to colour, that means the HUD's rims, panels and text should share ramps and darks, rather than each screen inventing a grey of its own.

How many greys a dark HUD needs

Design systems publish 11 or 12 grey steps because they cover every product and every hover and pressed state. Radix reserves three of its twelve steps just for interaction states [4], uses only two text greys [4], and gives three steps to borders [4]. Material 3's dark surfaces come down to five surface-container roles [21].

Stripping out the hover steps, a dark UI needs about six grey roles: background, raised panel, divider, disabled, secondary text and primary text. That list is an inference from the Radix roles rather than a published rule.

Two perceptual limits cap how many greys are worth having. A just-noticeable difference is roughly 2.3 in CIE76 [22], so greys closer than that look identical in play. And anything that marks a meaningful edge or state should reach 3:1 contrast against its neighbours [23]. In the narrow dark range from #101010 to #303030 that ratio cannot be met, so greys there can only be decorative layering, not separate signals.

Read this way, the 8-grey draft is exactly the six-role minimum once the three panel layers count as one background, and it is supported. The 11-grey draft is closer to a general-purpose library scale and is too many for one HUD.

It can go to 7. The text kit already has white at 45% for refused labels, which can also serve as secondary text, leaving paper only if a rim's light tone needs it. Going below 7 starts merging roles that do different jobs, such as divider and disabled. So your sense that 8 felt like a lot is fair, but the research floor is close: 7 is the leanest set that keeps every role.

How many colour meanings players can read

For colours that must be recognised at a glance, the guidance is consistently small. Visualisation guides recommend about six categorical colours, twelve at most [24]. Hand-picked categorical palettes usually stay under ten [25], and perception studies find a capacity limit near seven for colour alone [2].

That splits the HUD into two tiers:

  • The glance tier. Positive green, negative red, caution yellow, inactive grey, EXP blue and gold. HP bands reuse green, yellow and red, so this comes to about five or six hues, inside the limit.
  • The reference keys. The 18 type colours and the 18 or so status, stat and effect chips. Eighteen is far past fast recognition, so these work only as lookup keys that always carry a label, the way Pokemon type badges already do.

Pairing colour with a second cue measurably helps, most of all at five to eight categories [26]. Games also inherit conventions: loot rarity commonly runs white, green, blue, purple, orange [27], so green already carries "uncommon" for many players. That only matters if the game adds rarity tiers.

Readability and colour-blind safety in a bullet heaven

In a horde game the HUD competes with hundreds of sprites. Bullet-hell design advice is that enemy bullets must always stand out through bright, high-contrast colour [28], and shape carries meaning too, since round bullets do not show which way they are moving [29]. That argues for keeping saturated colour in the play field and the UI chrome muted, which the dark B2 panels already do.

Vampire Survivors shows the cost of getting it wrong. Players report that late runs become unreadable [30], and the game added an option to turn weapon effects off [31]. A similar setting is worth noting for later.

Colour vision deficiency affects about one in twelve men [32], mostly the red-green kind. The Game Accessibility Guidelines treat colour as a back-up channel [33], and Xbox requires at least two channels for important information [34].

For this HUD that means green for positive and red for negative should never be the only difference. HP bars already have bar length as a second channel. The focus rims need something more, such as an icon, a check or cross, a rim shape, or a clear brightness gap. The common alternative is a blue and orange pairing with icons [35].

Fit check: PokeSurvivor's palette

The board holds 141 unique colours: 54 fixed type colours, 28 status-chip colours and 59 UI colours, 18 of them greys [colours2-palette-snapshot.md](qw-file://workspace/artifacts/colours2-palette-snapshot.md).

Three near-duplicate pairs to settle

  • Fire red vs negative red are 2.3 apart, so players will see one colour. A Fire chip beside a negative button reads as "bad". Fire is fixed, so move negative red (darker or more crimson), or merge them and always pair negative with an icon.
  • Paralysis vs Lv yellow are also 2.3 apart, and the Lv yellow already doubles as HP middle. Treat it as one caution and gold colour on purpose; the chip labels keep the meanings apart.
  • EXP shade vs SpA shade are 3.1 apart, both dark tones of a blue. Merge them into one shade.

Two pink and green overlaps are worth a look as well. Bond pink and the Team Item pocket's pink, and berry green and positive green, either mean the same thing (merge them) or they don't (give them a shape or icon so they don't read alike).

A working rule for the board follows from all of this: add a colour only when it brings a new meaning, and merge any pair closer than about 3 in delta E. Shading inside a ramp can reuse the shared darks and lights rather than adding new ones, which is how pixel artists keep palettes small.

Suggested order on the board: apply the 8-grey fold (or the 7-grey version), settle the three near-duplicate pairs, then decide the red and green second cue. The All Colours tab can then show whether the UI count has landed near 30-35.

Gaps: no published colour counts were found for the HUDs of Vampire Survivors, Brotato, Halls of Torment, Celeste, Stardew Valley or Pokemon Black 2 / White 2, and IBM Carbon and GitHub Primer step counts were not checked. The 30-35 target is an inference from the sources above.
